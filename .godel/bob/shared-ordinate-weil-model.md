# The retained charge is not identified by an ordinate marginal and a finite Euler sum

Use $R,V,D,t,J,K,Z,P$ and the signed charge $H$ from [the phase-band test](weil-phase-bands.md). The following is a **local positive-density model**, not a zeta zero set. Two assignments of depths to the same ordinate density have the same analytic test integrals, with actual Euler coefficients through $2V$, yet lie on opposite sides of $H=0.233$. The separation holds for almost all odd squarefree $q\in[Q,2Q]$ as $Q\to\infty$, for sufficiently small fixed $\epsilon$. This is *not* an assertion of separation at failures. In particular the common marginal need not be the arithmetic marginal.

## Keeping both kinds of data fixed

Use $b=20/D$, eventually $2b<1/2$, and $\lambda=\log(t/(2\pi))/(2\pi)\sim R/(6\pi)$. At the line put density $\lambda l(x)$; at each side of depths $b,2b$ put respectively $\lambda m(x)/2,\lambda h(x)/2$ in the ordinate offset $x$. Thus the reflected sides are paired at identical $x$. Write $S_j f(x)=(f(x+ijb)+f(x-ijb))/2$ on analytic tests. Its Fourier multiplier is $C_j=\cosh(jb\xi)$ in angular frequency. Put

$$ v(x)=1-\frac1{\pi\lambda}\sum_{n\le 2V}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{\sqrt n}\cos((t+x)\log n),\qquad U=1/C_2,\quad W=C_1/C_2,\quad T=1/(2C_1+1). $$

The last three symbols also denote convolution operators. Write $\phi=Dx-2\arctan(2x)$, $F(u)=|\sum_{j=0}^{15}e^{iju}|^2/16$, $F_+=F(\phi)$, and $F_-=F(\phi+\pi)$. Use the following two assignments:

* B: $l=.84F_++ .07F_-$, $m=.07F_++ .005$, $h=U(v-l)-Wm$.
* A: To B add $(\delta,-(I+T)\delta,T\delta)$, where $\delta=.07(F_+-F_-)$. Thus $l_A=.91F_+$ and $m_A=.07F_-+.005-T\delta$.

Both have the identical pointwise ordinate marginal $\lambda(l+m+h)$. In both the pairing divided by $\lambda$ is

$$ \int(l f+m S_1f+h S_2f)\,dx=\int v f\,dx. \tag{1} $$

Here $f$ is holomorphic on a strip wider than $2b$ and decays as $O((1+|x|)^{-1-\eta})$ uniformly in a closed substrip wider than $2b$ for some $\eta>0$. Contour shifts give the symbols of $S_j$. The even kernels of $U,W,T$ are integrable, since their symbols are rescaled Schwartz functions. Move these kernels onto the shifted tests by Fubini. Then $US_2f=f$, $WS_2f=S_1f$, and $T(S_2-S_1)f=(S_1-I)f$ by $C_2-C_1=(2C_1+1)(C_1-1)$. This proves (1) without applying exponentially growing multipliers to densities. In particular, for the translated Poisson test $f=a/(a^2+(x-y)^2)$, $a=\sigma-1/2>2b$, the actual modeled Poisson sum is exactly

$$ \pi\lambda-\sum_{n\le2V}\Lambda(n)n^{-\sigma}\cos((t+y)\log n). $$

This follows from $S_j f=(k_{a+jb}+k_{a-jb})/2$ translated by $y$, and $\widehat k_a=\pi e^{-a|\xi|}$. It is the **finite** Euler model, not the infinite logarithmic derivative or an identity for the true gamma factor at every translation.

**Positivity.** For $M=U,W,T$ the convolution kernel has first absolute moment $O(b)$ by scaling. On each of the finitely many modes $e^{ik\phi}$ one may freeze the factor $e^{-2ik\arctan(2x)}$ inside the convolution, uniformly with error $O_k(b)$ by its derivative bound. Thus $M e^{ik\phi}=M(kD)e^{ik\phi}+O_k(b)$. For $k\ne0$ the symbol bounds are respectively $2e^{-40|k|},2e^{-20|k|},e^{-20|k|}$. Fejér coefficients are $1-|k|/16$ for $|k|<16$, zero otherwise. In particular $T\delta$ has limsup norm at most $.28/(e^{20}-1)$, since $\delta$ has no constant mode. The oscillatory parts of $Ul$ and $Wm$ in B have limsup norms at most $3.64/(e^{40}-1)$ and $.28/(e^{20}-1)$.

For $U(v-1)$ the part $n<R$ is $o(1)$ in supremum norm, since $\sum_{n\le X}\Lambda(n)/\sqrt n\sim2\sqrt X$ by the prime number theorem. For $n\ge R$, $2b\log n\ge20$, so the limsup norm of this part is at most $12\sqrt2\operatorname{sech}(20)<10^{-7}$. The constant left in $h_B$ is $1-.91-.075=.015$. Both models therefore have $.013<h<.017$ and $0<m<1.126$ for all $x$ eventually. The line densities are nonnegative. This positivity requires no condition on the phases of $n^{it}$.

**Size of the marginal discrepancy.** The common marginal even integrates to $\lambda |I|+O(\lambda/D)$ on *every* bounded real interval $I$, with constant independent of its length and position. In B its density divided by $\lambda$ is $(I_d-U)l+(I_d-W)m+Uv$ with $I_d$ the identity. The first two operators kill constants. Each nonconstant mode of $l,m$ integrates over any interval to $O(1/D)$: Integrate $e^{ik\phi}$ by parts with $\phi'=D-4/(1+4x^2)$ and $\int|\phi''|\,dx=8$. The same interval bound after $U,W$ follows by averaging translated intervals with kernels of bounded $L^1$ norm. In $U(v-1)$ use the bound $2/\log n$ for each integrated exponential. For $n<R$ the sum is $O(R^{-1/2})$ by the prime number theorem estimate and $\log n\ge\log 2$; for $n\ge R$ the factor $2/\log R=4/D$ suffices. This is a discrepancy about a local constant intensity, not an assertion that the ordinate marginal equals the true one. In particular an order-only count error of size $O(\log t/\log\log t)$ on local intervals would not rule out the model. No small implied constant is claimed.

## Evaluating the full and line charges

Let $f_D=g_\epsilon B^2$, $w(x)=g_\epsilon(x)/(1/4+x^2)$ on the real axis, and $A_\epsilon=(6\pi)^{-1}\int w$. The analytic test satisfies the strip condition in (1) for large $D$. On the line the two halves give

$$ f_D(x)=2w(x)(1+\cos\phi)+O(e^{-D/4}w(x)). $$

Indeed, $B=2\Re[(e^{ixD/2}-e^{-D/4})/(1/2+ix)]$. The constant part of $v$ in the full normalized charge $-\lambda\int v f_D/(2R)$ gives $-A_\epsilon+o(1)$ by oscillatory averaging. To see that the rest is $o(1)$ in density among the specified samples, it suffices to show convergence to zero in density for the real part of the sum of $n^{it}$ with coefficients

$$ d_n=\frac{\Lambda(n)}{R\sqrt n}\int f_D(x)e^{ix\log n}\,dx,\qquad n\le2V. $$

Freeze $R,D,V$ at $q=Q$ on $[Q,2Q]$, **only for this sampling estimate**. Uniformly $\Delta R=O(1)$, $\Delta D=O(1/R)$, and the $O(R)$ integers crossed by the cutoff cost $O(D/R)$ in total. The line formula for $B$ gives $\|\Delta f_D\|_1=O_\epsilon(1/R)$ using $|e^{ix\Delta D/2}-1|\le |x\Delta D|/2$ and $\int (1+|x|)w(x)\,dx<\infty$. The normalization change is also $o(1)$ by the prime number theorem estimate above. Thus the replacement costs $o(1)$ uniformly. The frozen polynomial has $\sum|d_n|=O(1)$ and $\sum|d_n|^2=O(D^3/R^2)$ by $\Lambda(n)\le\log n$. On the sampled height interval of length $\asymp Q^{3/5}$ (or its unit enlargement) its mean square is $O(D^3/R^2+V/Q^{3/5})$: Integrate cross terms using $|\log(n/m)|\gg 1/V$ for distinct indices. Its derivative is $O(D)$, and successive integer sample heights are spaced $\asymp Q^{-2/5}$. At any fixed bad threshold the bad samples force disjoint intervals of length $\gg Q^{-2/5}$ where at least half that threshold persists. The mean square shows there are $o(Q)$ such samples. Odd squarefrees have positive density, so restriction to them preserves convergence in density. All charges hereafter use the unfrozen cutoff.

The line charge in either model is $-\lambda\int lf_D/(2R)$. In the limit its factor multiplying $-A_\epsilon$ is the period mean of $l(1+\cos\phi)$: Each nonzero Fourier mode against $w$ vanishes by Riemann-Lebesgue applied with the slow phase in its amplitude. For A the mean is $.91+.91(15/16)=1.763125$; for B it is $.91+(.84-.07)(15/16)=1.631875$. Subtracting these line charges from the common full charge leaves **off-line signed** charges converging in density respectively to $.763125 A_\epsilon$ and $.631875 A_\epsilon$.

## Cutting to the exact bands

Both depths have fixed $c\in\{20,40\}$. On a fixed compact interval the two-half amplitude estimate gives $P=e^{ixD/2}\sqrt{g_\epsilon(x)}/(1/2+ix)+O(1/D)$, so the true gray region lies in $|\cos\phi|\le K/\cosh c+o(1)$ there. Use this only as an **outer cover**, since membership is defined using $2\arg P$. On actual gray entries $|\Re Z^2|\le2(1+K)|P|^2$. The compact limiting fraction of an enlarged carrier strip against $w$ is its angular fraction (approximate the indicator from above by continuous periodic functions, then shrink the enlargement). Moreover $|P|^2\to w$ uniformly in modulus-squared difference on compact sets, and $|P|^2\ll(1+x^2)^{-1}$ globally. The same integrable tail bound, with a fixed depth-dependent constant, applies to the full pair summands. Thus the central hole and $|x|>J$ cost $o(1)$, and the limsup absolute gray cost is at most

$$ (1+K)A_\epsilon\sum_{(c,d)=(20,1.126),(40,.017)} d\,\frac2\pi\arcsin(K/\cosh c)<10^{-7}. $$

Here the pair density is $\lambda m/2$ or $\lambda h/2$, not the density on both sides added together. The crude last bound follows from $A_\epsilon\le1/3$, $K<1.05$, $\operatorname{sech}(20)<10^{-8}$, and $\arcsin u\le2u$ for $0\le u\le1/2$. This estimates the error in evaluating the original **signed difference**, not a favorable-only surrogate. Finally $A_\epsilon\to1/3$ by dominated convergence as $\epsilon\downarrow0$. The corresponding off-line benchmarks $.254375$ and $.210625$ straddle $.233$ with room for this gray error. Fix $\epsilon$ first, then use the convergence in density. No exact limiting value for the gray charge is required.

**What persists at exceptional samples?** Without any sampling argument the full charges in A and B cancel in their difference. The preceding line calculation and gray bounds thus give $H_A-H_B=.13125 A_\epsilon+e$, $\limsup|e|\le 2\cdot10^{-7}$, at *all* moving heights. More precisely, let $S_V(t)=V^{-1}\sum_{p<V}(\log p)\log(V/p)p^{it}$. In either model the arithmetic part of the full charge is $\Re S_V(t)+O(\epsilon)+o(1)$ uniformly. Indeed its coefficient at $n\le2V$ before the cosine is $\Lambda(n)(K_0*k_\epsilon)(\log n)/(R\sqrt n)$, where $K_0$ is the real two-half autocorrelation (not the phase threshold $K$). This is the inverse Fourier transform of $g_\epsilon B^2$ in the convention with factor $1/(2\pi)$. Apply the [absolute prime-side smoothing error](short-buffered-packet.md#price-the-smoothing-on-the-original-prime-side) just to $n\le2V$: Its $O(\epsilon)$ majorant remains valid on this subset, and the unsmoothed $K_0$ vanishes above $V$. The same-half and prime-power corrections there are $o(1)$ even in absolute sum. Thus $H_A$ and $H_B$ equal $\Re S_V(t)$ plus their respective off-line benchmarks above, up to $O(\epsilon)+o(1)$ and the gray error. On a hypothetical high-endpoint failure the real peak is $\ge47/81-o(1)$, so **both** models are then on the high side, not on opposite sides. Matching the failing first harmonic itself in the finite data does not invalidate the high-charge assignment. This is a conditional test at its own cutoff, not evidence that the peak occurs.

## If integer ordinate counts are required

There is a discrete version with exactly the same counting marginal in the two assignments but only **approximate** analytic data. Write $A_i,B_j$ for the three unscaled depth densities in the respective assignments, and $M=\sum A_i=\sum B_j>0$. Use nine joint *pair* densities $\lambda A_i B_j/(2M)$. Round each joint density by placing an atom at integer levels of its cumulative integral based at zero (use one generalized inverse per level in its range). On any bounded interval the count differs from its mass by $O(1)$; endpoint conventions only affect the absolute constant. In A give this atom depth $i$, and in B give it depth $j$, in each case as a same-ordinate reflected pair with a double line entry at depth zero. Row and column sums give exactly the desired continuous pair masses before rounding. Thus the two discrete ordinate marginals agree exactly, including multiplicities. Their interval discrepancy relative to $\lambda |I|$ is still $O(1+\lambda/D)$.

Integration by parts costs $O(\int |F'|)$ per cell for a smooth decaying test trace $F$. For the Poisson kernels at $1<\sigma\le2$ this is $O(1)$ uniformly in translation since all widths are eventually bounded below by $1/4$. For $g_\epsilon B^2$ on the three depth traces the variation is $O_\epsilon(D)$: In the two-half formula the exponential carriers have bounded moduli (here $c\le40$) and derivatives $O(D)$, the two denominator real parts exceed $1/4$ eventually, and the multiplier and its derivative decay integrably. Hence the full and line charge errors are $O_\epsilon(D/R)$. For gray charge, use the same outer strip on a fixed compact. It has $O(D)$ interval components because $\phi$ is monotone with derivative $D+O(1)$. For the upper envelope on these components use $w+o(1)$ uniformly and integrate $w$ by parts on each, losing $O(D/R)$ after normalization, not a bound based on differentiating exact membership. Outside the compact, the integrable envelope $(1+x^2)^{-1}$ with the same interval discrepancy prices both tails. The central hole costs $O(1/D+1/R)$. The strict threshold separation therefore survives rounding. This does not give exact finite Euler traces for atoms; the exact data assertion concerns the continuous model only.

## Restoring a global density bound at sparse centers

The density-one statement for **separately built** local models does not put blocks at a positive fraction of heights in one global model. Nevertheless a global density theorem alone does not repair identification at a prescribed exception. Here is a sparse version, in the spirit of the [individual projection model](individual-projection-model.md), now for these two assignments and their finite Euler data.

Select good odd squarefrees $q_n$ from the strict threshold separation with fixed slack, so far apart that $R_{n+1}>4R_n$, starting with $40/D_1<1/8$. Around $t_n=2q_n^{3/5}$ keep the rounded assignments just on $|\gamma-t_n|\le R_n^2$, and copy their conjugates at negative ordinates. In the rest of the positive axis use identical line atoms in both sets, obtained by rounding the background $(2\pi)^{-1}\log(\max(2\pi e,\gamma)/(2\pi))$ and restricting it to the complement; reflect to negative ordinates. This preserves the common marginal exactly. The retained sums at the designated samples are **unchanged** because the actual annuli are wholly within their blocks. Only $O(R_n^3)$ positive off-line atoms occur in the $n$th block, by the marginal interval estimate. Consequently the total positive-ordinate off-line count through $T$ is $O(\log^3(2+T))$, summing the geometric series including a partially entered block at $t_n\asymp T$ or below. All are at $\beta<5/8$ on the right. In particular this is compatible asymptotically with the global Ingham bounds used in [the depth estimate](deep-retained-excess.md), uniformly even at the moving depth. For the *total* positive-ordinate count the error relative to the integrated background is $O(1+\log(2+T)/\log\log(3+T))$: In each full or partial block the integral of the background differs from $\lambda_n$ times length by $O(R_n^4/t_n)$, and the marginal discrepancy costs $O(1+R_n/D_n)$. The sum is dominated by its last term (enlarge the spacings if needed); rounding the background on the gaps adds only $O(1)$ per gap. These are order bounds, not arithmetic equalities or bounds with prescribed small constants.

For $|y|\le R_n^2/2$ and $1<\sigma\le2$, the Poisson sum at $t_n+y$ still gives the finite Euler trace for that block up to $O(1)$. Besides rounding, the change is bounded by the two tails at distance $\ge R_n^2/2$. The original rounded block continued indefinitely has $O(R_n)$ atoms per unit interval. The global sets have $O(\log(2+|\gamma|))$ per unit interval, by the same local bound in each block and the background elsewhere. Summing inverse-square tails using $\log(2+|\gamma|)\le\log(2+|t_n+y|)+\log(2+|\gamma-t_n-y|)$ costs $O((R_n+\log R_n)/R_n^2)$. No exact analytic identity after truncating blocks or quantizing atoms is asserted. In particular this restores sparse off-line counts, not a true Euler product between the designated neighborhoods. The selected conductors are still not failing twists.

## A single interior trace does distinguish the assignments

As a test of *which missing datum matters*, move just one real logarithmic-derivative trace inside the depth layers. Take $a=1/D$ and let $I_a(y)$ denote the model sum of $k_{1/2+a-\beta}(x-y)$, now using the **signed** numerator even to the right of this vertical. No layer of this model is on the vertical. The difference $(I_{a,A}-I_{a,B})/(\pi\lambda)$ equals a convolution applied to $\delta$, with symbol at $u=|\xi|$ given by

$$ m_a(u)=e^{-au}+\sinh(au)\big[(1+T(u))e^{-bu}-T(u)e^{-2bu}\big]. $$

Indeed a reflected pair layer at distance $jb>a$ has symbol $-\pi e^{-jbu}\sinh(au)$ per unit marginal, whereas the line has symbol $\pi e^{-au}$. This sign change is why (1), whose tests must be holomorphic across all the layers, does not already give the interior trace. The symbol at $u=D$ is at least $e^{-1}$. The kernels in this calculation are linear combinations of Poisson kernels of widths proportional to $1/D$, possibly convolved with $T$. They have uniformly bounded $L^1$ norms and uniformly vanishing absolute mass outside every fixed neighborhood of zero. Thus the slow phase on each of the finitely many modes can be frozen with uniform $o(1)$ error, by uniform continuity; no first absolute moment of a Poisson kernel is required. Project onto $\cos\phi$ with weight $w/\int w$. Orthogonality in the oscillatory limit as before leaves the first Fourier coefficient of $\delta$, namely $.14(15/16)=.13125$, multiplied by $m_a(D)$. In particular

$$ \liminf_{q\to\infty}\frac{\int w(y)\cos\phi(y)(I_{a,A}(y)-I_{a,B}(y))\,dy}{\pi\lambda\int w}>.048. $$

This uses **only** the extra interior trace as the distinguishing datum. Even in the local rounded models the additional pointwise error after normalization is $O(D/R)$ by Poisson variation at minimum width $1/D$. For actual zeros there is no such minimum width at this test vertical. The calculation neither bounds the interior logarithmic derivative of zeta nor proves that a single interior trace determines the retained charge for general depth distributions.

**Scope.** The common analytic functional determines the *full* charge, but subtracting the critical line depends on which depths carry the ordinate density. These local models match the stated marginal to each other and match (1) exactly, not approximately; they do not impose actual zero locations or an exceptional prime phase condition. The sparse version adds global count bounds while weakening the exact analytic identities. Even the rounded pairs are not proposed zeros. Applying more translations or widths to the same finite analytic data cannot by itself resolve the missing allocation.
