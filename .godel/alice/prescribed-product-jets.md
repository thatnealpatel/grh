# Confine two prescribed row products with one fat point

At a complete first input $v$ of the [actual two-label list](same-input-second-bracket.md), prescribe **one second cofactor** $k$. Count candidate columns with $h=k$, so that both $v=cx_1$ and $v_2=cx_2$, $x_1=F_1a$, $x_2=F_2k$, are fixed. All the estimates below hold on any subfamily, including outside the [chosen denominator-pair region](denominator-pair-gap-regions.md) when a partner is specified. This is not a claim that a single $k$ per input captures the triples. The saving here is to confine the actual two gaps even though the large divisor common to $v-q\alpha$ and $v_2-q\beta$ varies.

## The first divisor and the jet lattice

Use $s=.2552$. In $d=q^{1.055+o(1)}$ take a smooth divisor $f$ first crossing $q^s$ in a fixed ordering. The prime cutoff $X=q^{o(1)}$ bounds the overshoot. Enumerate **all** these $f$ at cost $q^{s/2+o(1)}$ by the list's smooth half-power bound; no uniform distribution in the filters is assumed. Since $q$ is a unit modulo $f$, both gaps have fixed centered residues $\alpha_f,\beta_f$ there. Write the gaps as $(\alpha_f+fi,\beta_f+fj)$, where $|i|,|j|\le N=q^{.390-s+o(1)}$. Put

$$ A=(v-q\alpha_f)/f,\quad B=(v_2-q\beta_f)/f,\quad P=(A,B,q),\quad l=d/f.$$

The integral vector $P$ is primitive since $q$ is prime and $v$ is a unit modulo $q$. The affine point $(i,j)$ satisfies $P-q(i,j,1)=l(Y,Z,0)$, *without* needing $(l,f)=1$. Each $(i,j)$ costs at most divisor-many $d$, since $l\mid A-qi>0$ at an actual point. In particular arbitrary solutions of two congruences are not counted as triples.

Here is the covolume used to produce a single curve **before** enumerating $l$ or the points. Fix integers $1\le r\le m$. Let $K$ be the coefficient lattice of integral ternary degree-$m$ forms vanishing at $[P]$ to order at least $r$. Use Euclidean coefficient norm after multiplying the coefficient of $X^iY^jZ^{m-i-j}$ by $N^{i+j}$. Write

$$ n=\binom{m+2}{2},\quad t=\binom{r+1}{2},\quad S=\binom{m+2}{3},\quad b=\binom{r+1}{3},\quad L=t(m-r+1). $$

The rank is $n-t$, and the covolume in its real span is, up to constants depending only on $m,r$,

$$ N^{2(S-b)}\big((A/N)^2+(B/N)^2+q^2\big)^{L/2}. \tag{1} $$

To see the normalization, use the dual action $R(g)$ on coefficient functionals: $(R(g)\ell)(F)=\ell(F\circ g)$. At $e_3$ the first jets are just the $t$ coordinate readers with $i+j<r$; let $W_0$ be their exterior product. Extend the primitive $P$ to the last column of $g\in\mathrm{SL}_3(\mathbb Z)$. Substitution and its inverse are integral, so $W=(\bigwedge^t R(g))W_0$ is the wedge of an integral basis of the full annihilator of $K$. Vanishing is intrinsic in the projective chart. In particular there is no gcd of minors to charge at $P$.

For any real invertible $h$ fixing the line of $e_3$, write its last column as $\lambda e_3$ and its upper left block as $M$. In successive layers $i+j=k<r$ the jet action is triangular by total degree, with diagonal block the degree-$k$ symmetric power of $M$ (up to transpose convention) times $\lambda^{m-k}$. This follows by substituting $h(x,y,1)=(M(x,y),\lambda+u x+w y)$ in a monomial. The block has size $k+1$ and symmetric-power determinant $(\det M)^{k(k+1)/2}$, by triangularization. Thus the absolute multiplier on $W_0$ is $|\det h|^b |\lambda|^{mt-3b}=|\det h|^b |\lambda|^L$. For general $h$, rotate $h e_3$ onto its axis first: Rotations and their inverses act with bounded norm in this fixed degree. Hence the wedge norm is comparable to $|\det h|^b\|h e_3\|^L$.

Finally the coefficient scaling $D$ has determinant $N^{2S}$ (sum of either variable's exponents). A primitive kernel-basis wedge is the Hodge dual of $W$ up to sign: Both lattices are saturated complementary orthogonal lattices over the rationals, and the star takes primitive integral wedges to primitive ones. Under scaling its volume becomes $|\det D|\|(\bigwedge^t D^{-T})W\|$. The dual scaling is $R(\operatorname{diag}(1/N,1/N,1))$, so the preceding calculation with $h=\operatorname{diag}(1/N,1/N,1)g$ proves (1).

## A strict integer margin

Since $|A|,|B|\le q^{2.500-s+o(1)}$, (1) has exponent at most $E=2(S-b)(.390-s)+2.110 L$. Minkowski in $K$ supplies a nonzero integral form $F$ of weighted norm at most $q^{E/(n-t)+o(1)}$. At every actual point, the integral Taylor expansion at $P$ gives $l^r\mid F(qi,qj,q)$: Terms of order less than $r$ in the first two coordinate differences vanish, and both differences are divisible by $l$. As $(q,l)=1$, this gives $l^r\mid F(i,j,1)$. The ordinary absolute value there is bounded by a fixed constant times the weighted norm. It follows that $F(i,j,1)=0$ throughout the filter if

$$ E < (1.055-s)r(n-t). \tag{2} $$

For $s=.2552$, use $(m,r)=(2554,647)$. Here $n-t=3055662$, $L=399970224$, and the two sides of (2) times $100000$ are respectively $158121507599040$ and $158121524853720$. These large degrees are fixed independently of $q$; this is an asymptotic existence proof, not an efficient construction. For an easier confinement alone, $s=.272$, $(m,r)=(33,8)$ gives $3499756<3501576$ after multiplying (2) by $1000$, but paying for the larger first divisor leaves less room for a point count.

## Price all but small components at the jet point

Factor $F$ over $\mathbb Q$ and use primitive integral factors $C$. Their coefficients have polynomial height: In fixed degree $\|CD\|\gg\|C\|\|D\|$ for ordinary coefficient norms by compactness of the unit spheres, and a nonzero integral cofactor has norm at least one. The number of components is bounded independently of $q$. If $C(P)\ne0$, any represented $l$ divides this nonzero polynomial-height integer by evaluation modulo $l$. Each resulting $d$ specifies at most one actual column at this $v$. If a rationally irreducible component splits geometrically, its rational points lie on two distinct conjugate factors, hence cost $O_m(1)$ points by Bézout (characteristic zero). Ignore the line at infinity.

A line through $P$ contains at most one distinct actual affine point in the box. Otherwise write it as $uX+wY+zZ=0$ with $|u|,|w|\ll N$, $|z|\ll N^2$ by crossing two such points. Its equation at $P$ gives $c(u x_1+w x_2)=q(u\alpha_f+w\beta_f-z f)$. Thus $q\mid u x_1+w x_2$, whereas its magnitude is $\ll q f N^2/c<q$. If it vanishes, nonzero $(u,w)$ requires one entry of size at least $\min(x_1,x_2)/(x_1,x_2)\ge q^{1.330-o(1)}$ by [row freshness](shared-row-labels.md), impossible.

Through-point conics also cost $q^{o(1)}$ per filter. Here are the two cases; the repeated direction at infinity needs a different argument from a Pell bound. Write the affine conic as $Q(i,j)+b_1i+b_2j+b_0=0$.

* If the binary quadratic part $Q$ is nonsingular, translate by the rational center, and clear denominators to obtain $Q(I,J)=K$ for integral translated, scaled variables, all of polynomial height. The translation is injective, since the Hessian is invertible, and $K\ne0$ by geometric irreducibility. After a bounded integral shear ensure the coefficient $a$ of $I^2$ is nonzero. Completing the square gives $U^2-\Delta J^2=4aK\ne0$, with $U=2aI+bJ$, $b$ the mixed coefficient, and $\Delta\ne0$ the discriminant of $Q$. For square $\Delta$ use integer factor pairs. Otherwise the algebraic integer $U+\sqrt\Delta J$ in the quadratic field has nonzero norm $4aK$; its integral ideal divides $(4aK)$, with at most $\operatorname{divcount}(|4aK|)^4$ possibilities by prime ideal factorization. Generators of any one ideal differ by units. Both embeddings have absolute value between $q^{-O_m(1)}$ and $q^{O_m(1)}$ by the norm equation and height, so this costs only $O_m(1+\log q)$ units: In the real case consecutive distinct absolute sizes have ratio bounded away from one since a real quadratic unit of absolute size $u>1$ has $u+1/u$ or $u-1/u$ integral, and in the imaginary case there are at most six units. Thus this count is uniform in the conic.
* For a repeated direction write $Q=a(ui+wj)^2$ with primitive integral $(u,w)$ and nonzero integer $a$. If there are at least five different points, the five evaluations on $1,i,j,i^2,ij,j^2$ have rank five by Bézout. The signed five-by-five minors therefore give a multiple of the primitive conic. Omitting any quadratic monomial leaves total degree six, so all coefficients of $Q$ are $O(N^6)=o(q)$. In particular $q\nmid a$, and $|u|,|w|\ll N^3$. Vanishing at $P$ gives $q\mid uA+wB$ by reduction modulo $q$. Divide the homogeneous equation by $q$ and reduce again: $q\mid b_1A+b_2B$. Since $(A,B)\not\equiv(0,0)\pmod q$, the linear forms $(u,w)$ and $(b_1,b_2)$ are proportional modulo $q$, with $(u,w)$ nonzero modulo $q$. For an integral point $T=ui+wj$ now satisfies a nonzero quadratic congruence $aT^2+\lambda T+b_0=0\pmod q$, while $|T|\ll N^4=o(q)$. There are at most two such integers. Each determines at most one point since $ub_2-wb_1\ne0$ in characteristic zero (otherwise the conic splits geometrically). This contradicts five points, so at most four are possible. In particular, treating all parabolas as Pell equations was unnecessary.

For an absolutely irreducible component of degree $D\ge13$ the uniform [Bombieri–Pila integral-point bound, Theorem 5](https://people.maths.ox.ac.uk/pila/Ovals.pdf) on the translated square gives at most $N^{1/D+o(1)}$ distinct points, irrespective of singularities. Including all $f$, this costs at most $q^{z+o(1)}$ columns, where

$$ z=.1276+.1348/13=1121/8125<.138. $$

There is also a price below $q^z$ for components of degree $7\le D\le12$ **smooth at $P$**, with no good-reduction assumption on all the prime factors of $l$. Use affine coordinates at $P/q$. Call primes bad if they divide $q$ or the numerator of one *chosen nonzero* affine first derivative there of $C(X,Y,1)$. This integer has polynomial height; the bad support has $O_m(\log q/\log\log q)$ primes. Enumerate the full bad-supported part $l_0$ at subpower cost: For any fixed $0<\epsilon<1$, the count up to $q^2$ is bounded by $q^{2\epsilon}$ times the partial Euler product at $\epsilon$ on this support. Even for the first $O_m(\log q)$ primes its log is $O_{m,\epsilon}((\log q)^{1-\epsilon})=o(\log q)$ by summing over their indices. Let $\epsilon$ tend to zero in the exponent bound. If $l_0>2N$, the congruence to $P/q$ fixes at most one point in the box. Otherwise $l/l_0\ge q^{1.055-s-.1348-o(1)}$. Choose a smooth $g\mid l/l_0$ first crossing $q^\tau$, $\tau=.0194>.1348/7$. All choices per $f,C,l_0$ cost $q^{\tau/2+o(1)}$.

For clarity, at each fixed $g$ only boundedly many points remain. Take a degree-compatible monomial order, and all bivariate monomials through degree $M$ not divisible by the leading monomial of $C(X,Y,1)$. There are $a=DM+O_D(1)$ of them, with sum of degrees $DM^2/2+O_D(M)$ by subtracting the shifted triangle. They are independent modulo $C$. Every $a$-row square evaluation determinant has size $\le a!N^{DM^2/2+O_D(M)}$. For $p^k\parallel g$ the two coordinates are congruent to $P/q$ modulo $p^k$. In $\mathbb Z_p$ the chosen derivative is a unit. After translating by $P/q$ and possibly exchanging coordinates, the curve is given on this residue disk by an integral power series in the first displacement: Solve recursively for its coefficients using the unit first partial with respect to the second coordinate. The series converges at displacements in $p\mathbb Z_p$, and two solutions in the disk for the same first displacement agree by subtracting the equations and factoring out their second-coordinate difference with a unit quotient. Consequently each monomial evaluation column expands in powers of a parameter in $p^k\mathbb Z_p$ with integral coefficients. Multilinearity on truncated series, followed by passage to the limit, gives $p^{k\binom a2}$ in the determinant: Nonzero terms need distinct powers. Multiplying over primes gives $g^{\binom a2}$. For sufficiently large **fixed** $M$, $\tau D>.1348$ makes this exceed the archimedean bound for all sufficiently large $q$. All determinants vanish, giving a nonzero rational linear combination vanishing on the points (also if fewer than $a$). It is not divisible by $C$ by the monomial choice, so Bézout bounds the points by $DM$. The total cost exponent including $f$ is $.1276+.0194/2=.1373<z$.

## Which price sums

Call a candidate priced if on its filter it lies on any component counted above. The others are confined, at each prescribed $v,k,f$, to boundedly many absolutely irreducible through-$P$ curves of degrees $3$ to $12$, with degrees $7$ to $12$ necessarily singular at $P$. No estimate for occupancy of those remaining curves is implied by their existence.

* For **one** prescribed cofactor per input, priced columns cost $q^{.860+z+o(1)}$ on the list. Ordered triples with *both* columns priced there and both using that prescribed cofactor cost at most $q^{.860+2z+o(1)}$, with $.860+2z<1.13594$. Restricting to failure of the maximizing pair region only decreases this count.
* If instead each actual reference prescribes its own $k$ and its partners must use that $k$, the priced partners cost $q^{z+o(1)} I'$ after summing over the $I'$ actual retained reference columns. This linear term would be affordable in the incidence Cauchy inequality since $.860+z<.998$, but is **not** by itself a $q^{1.136-\eta}$ triple bound. Make any column deletions before forming this sum.
* The point and its jet really do change with an unrestricted candidate $h$: A free half-power sum over $h\asymp q^{.665}$ adds $.3325$, not $o(1)$. But some varying $h$ can be paid for **jointly with the first divisor**. At a fixed $v$ prescribe one support $S$ of $O(\log q)$ primes, for instance the coordinates of one actual reference. For each eligible $n$ write $n_S$ for its full supported part. Allow every $h$ with $h/h_S\le\min(d_S,q^s)$. First enumerate $U=d_S$ and $h_S$ at subpower cost by the support count above. If $U<q^s$, include all of $U$ in $f$ and cross $q^s/U$ in $d/U$: There are at most $q^{o(1)}\sqrt{q^s/U}$ possible extra divisors, and at most $q^{o(1)}\sqrt U$ possible outside parts of $h$. If $U\ge q^s$, cross $q^s$ inside $U$ in specified order at no further price, and the outside part costs at most $q^{s/2+o(1)}$. Thus all $(f,h)$ together still cost $q^{s/2+o(1)}$; the pointwise bounds at each $v,h,f$ are unchanged. Use this modified filter rule to define priced columns for this support test. With one such $S$ per input, **even with different second cofactors**, triples with both columns passing the support and component tests cost $q^{.860+2z+o(1)}$. Using all references instead, to test only the candidate against the reference support, costs $q^{z+o(1)} I'$ for passing partners. This does not cover candidates with large new cofactor parts or bound the full complement.

A next arithmetic test is whether the smooth outputs $Y=(A-qi)/l$, $Z=(B-qj)/l$ restrict occupancy on the remaining through-point cubics, even when the second input product is prescribed. Pointwise Bombieri–Pila on a cubic alone costs $.1276+.1348/3$, and a smooth-point divisor crossing at $\tau>.1348/3$ still costs more than $.138$ including $f$. A solution there would improve the prescribed-cofactor branch, not justify summing it over all second products. The unrestricted maximizing-region complement is open.
